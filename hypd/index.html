Brand thinking

How I understand a brand

Good content starts before the first word. This is how I work out what a brand should say, to whom, and where.

  1. 01

    Start with the objective

    What does the brand need right now?

    New people to the category, people switching from a competitor, or existing users coming back more often. Every other choice follows from this.

  2. 02

    Know the person

    Who are we talking to, and what’s stopping them?

    I define the audience by need and moment, not just age, then list what pushes them to act (triggers) and what holds them back (barriers).

  3. 03

    Find the one thing to say

    What should they believe after seeing this?

    I write it as one line: “I want to convince this audience that…”. It moves them from what they think today to what we want them to think.

  4. 04

    Go from features to feelings

    Why should they care?

    Product features lead to practical benefits, and those lead to how it makes people feel. I back every claim with a reason to believe: proof, data or a trusted voice.

  5. 05

    Match the stage and channel

    Where are they in the journey?

    Awareness, consideration, first try, repeat and recommendation each need different content, spread across paid, owned and earned media.

  6. 06

    Measure what matters

    Did it move the objective?

    If the goal is installs, I track installs, not just views. The numbers decide what we make next.

In practice: at Signal the goal was app installs, so every creator got a tracked link and we judged them on installs and sign-ups, not just views. At TechGig, articles built awareness while webinars and newsletters moved readers closer to signing up.

My workflow

How I work

Six steps, every month.

  1. Spot the moment

    I track what’s coming and what’s trending, so content is ready before the moment arrives.

  2. Pick the format

    Every moment becomes something people can join: a quiz, a debate, a tribute or a meme.

  3. Plan the month

    A day-by-day calendar across platforms, with one idea reshaped for each.

  4. Write the hook first

    The first two seconds decide if anyone watches, so the hook comes first. Every fact gets checked.

  5. Brief the team

    Designers and editors get one clear brief per post, so there are fewer revisions.

  6. Track and improve

    Results go into a tracker. What works gets more space next month.

Methods I use

The frameworks behind every calendar, with real examples from my work.

Reach → Engage → Convert

Every post has one job. Memes and trends bring new people in, quizzes and debates make them interact, and demos and user stories turn them into users.

Example: office memes for reach, the Kapil Dev quiz for comments, and "Signal Ki Kahaani" user testimonials to convert.

Hero, Hub, Hygiene

Big moments (hero), recurring series people come back for (hub), and useful everyday posts that keep the page alive (hygiene).

Example: the Ballon d'Or series was a hero moment, Guess the Player and This or That were hubs, and daily match schedules and WhatsApp polls were hygiene.

Keep the sell small

People follow a page because it gives them something. Most posts entertain or inform; only a small share asks for the download.

35%25%20%12%8%

Example: my monthly mix, left to right: Predict & Win 35%, Match Day 25%, Know Your Sport 20%, Fan culture 12%, and only 8% direct "download the app" posts (the yellow block).

Series beat one-offs

A recurring format builds habit. People know what to expect and come back for the next episode.

Example: the Ballon d'Or ran as three episodes in the build-up to the award night instead of one post.

Match the metric to the format

Different posts win in different ways, so I judge each one by the right number.

Example: quizzes are judged on comments, memes on shares and reach, carousels on saves, and collabs on new followers.

Every post asks for a reply

End with a question anyone can answer in one word or one pick. Comments push the post to more people.

Example: "Who would you put at No. 1? 👇" and "Apna score comments mein batao."

SEO & content strategy

My SEO playbook

This is the approach I built and ran at TechGig, and the one I bring to any brand that wants traffic from people who are ready to act.

  1. Target people ready to act

    I sort every keyword by what the searcher wants to do. Most effort goes to topics where readers are close to a decision.

    High intent · focusHiring, salary, how to apply

    E.g. Infosys hiring 2026

    Medium intent · supportTrends, AI and jobs, layoffs

    Leads readers deeper

    Low intent · skipBasic definitions

    Views, but no action

  2. Research from several sources

    Google autocomplete and related searches, Google Trends, Search Console, industry news and competitor gaps. One tool alone misses too much.

    At TechGig: Google Trends flagged spikes in hiring topics, so articles were planned around hiring cycles.

  3. Write for Google and AI answers

    Every article follows one template, so it ranks on Google and gets picked up by AI answer tools like ChatGPT and Google AI Overviews (this is called AEO, answer engine optimisation).

    Keyword headline→40–50 word summary at the top→Who, what, why now in 3 lines→Scannable sections→What this means for you→3–5 FAQs

  4. Make every article lead somewhere

    2–4 internal links per article, in a loop that keeps readers moving through the site. No article is left on its own.

    Hiring→Career guide→Upskilling→Hiring

  5. Get found faster

    A Google News listing so articles can show in Discover and Top Stories, schema markup (code that tells Google what a page is about), updated sitemaps, short keyword URLs and fast-loading pages.

  6. Set one standard for the team

    I wrote the writing guide (SOP) for the team. Before writing, every writer answers one question: what should the reader do next? Apply, prepare, learn or decide. A final checklist catches weak drafts before they go live.

  7. Measure, refresh, repeat

    Google Analytics (GA4) and Search Console show what's working. Winning formats get repeated, articles stuck at 200–500 views get refreshed, and newsletter subject lines are tested in two versions to see which gets more opens.

Case study 01

HealthKart · Whey protein for first-time gym-goers

The brief

  • Audience: 20 to 28 year olds who joined a gym in the last few months
  • Channel: Instagram and Facebook feed ads
  • Goal: a first purchase from someone who has never bought protein

The insight

  • Is it fake? Everyone has heard a story about fake supplements.
  • Is it safe? Someone at home thinks protein means steroids.
  • Is it working? They’re sore, tired and close to quitting.

The idea: stop selling "the best protein". Answer one worry per ad.

Ad 1PAS: Problem, Agitate, SolveInstagram feed ad

Current

“Buy 100% authentic whey protein at the best price. Shop now!”

After

Headline: Is your protein even real?

Text: You can’t spot a fake tub by looking at it. Most people only find out after two months of zero progress and an upset stomach. Buy from somewhere you can actually verify, then go worry about your deadlift.

Button: Shop verified protein

Spec

HealthKart

Why this framework

PAS works best when the buyer already carries a fear. I don’t have to invent a problem here. Fake supplements are something every Indian gym-goer has heard about, so the ad just names it and offers a way out.

What I changed
  • Every brand says "100% authentic". Nobody believes a claim they’ve seen fifty times, so I turned it into a question.
  • "Two months of zero progress" makes the cost of a fake tub real. That’s two months gone, and that hurts more than the money.
  • The last line ends on a joke. A beginner reading this is nervous, and a little lightness lowers their guard.

Why this framework

Sometimes the buyer is sold but someone else has a veto. In a lot of Indian homes, that’s a parent who thinks protein powder is steroids. Answer their doubt and the sale gets a lot easier.

What I changed
  • "No side effects" sounds like a legal disclaimer, and it puts the word "side effects" in the reader’s head. I cut it.

One ad, four headlines

The 4U test: a headline should be useful, urgent, unique or ultra-specific. I write a handful, then let the numbers pick.

Useful

Build muscle without guessing what’s in the tub

Urgent

Joined the gym this month? Get protein right from week one

Unique

The protein your mom will also approve of

Ultra-specific

One scoop after your workout. That’s the whole plan.

How I’d test it: same image, same audience, two headlines, one week. Keep the one that brings in purchases at a lower cost. Clicks alone can fool you.

Case study 02

Rapido · Bike taxis for the daily office commute

The brief

  • Audience: office-goers aged 22 to 35 in Bengaluru and Delhi
  • Channels: feed ad, push notification, billboard and app store
  • Goal: get people to try a bike taxi for their daily commute

The insight

  • The commute isn’t really about distance. It’s about not knowing.
  • Will the auto agree to go? Will the cab show up? Will I be late again?
  • Every format has a different few seconds to win, so each one gets a different framework.

The idea: sell certainty, and let the speed speak for itself.

Piece 1AIDA: Attention, Interest, Desire, ActionInstagram feed ad

Current

“Book affordable bike taxi rides with Rapido. Fast, safe and convenient.”

After

Headline: 9:52. Meeting at 10.

Text: The cab app says 18 minutes. The traffic says longer. A bike gets through gaps a car can’t, and you walk in with time to grab water.

Button: Book a bike

Spec

Rapido

Why this framework

A feed ad gets about a second before the thumb moves on, so it has to grab first and sell last. AIDA keeps that order: the clock grabs attention, the cab ETA everyone has stared at builds interest, walking in on time is the desire, and the button is the action.

What I changed
  • "Fast, safe and convenient" could describe any app on the phone. A time on a clock is something every office-goer has felt in their chest.
  • I never say "fast". The reader works it out from the story, which is more convincing.
  • The copy never lists a feature. The problem does the selling.

Why this framework

On the app store, people are already comparing options and want reasons. FAB lays them out in order: what you get, why it’s better, and what that means for your day.

What I changed
  • Feature: three ride types in one app. Advantage: bikes skip traffic. Benefit: your evening back.
  • "Low prices" became "usually cost less than a cab". A comparison is easier to believe than a vague promise.
  • It ends on how your evening feels, because that’s what people remember when they come back to compare apps.

The rules I write by

  • Start with the reader’s problem, not the brand
  • One ad, one message
  • Keep asking "so what?" until a feature becomes a feeling
  • Write the way the audience talks, Hinglish included
  • If a word doesn’t earn its place, cut it
  • The button should say what happens next

These are spec pieces. Any product claim would be checked against the brand’s brief and data before going live.
